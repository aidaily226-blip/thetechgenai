Singapore AI Robots 2026: From Dental Surgery to Prison Patrols and Smart Cities

Something interesting is happening in Singapore.

The country is not only investing in artificial intelligence that generates text or answers questions. It is also exploring how AI-powered machines can operate in hospitals, public spaces, prisons, and industrial environments.

A robot that assists with dental implant surgery. An autonomous machine that patrols a prison. Humanoid robots being trained to help officers investigate dangerous scenes. Delivery and cleaning robots being tested in a smart urban district.

These developments show how Singapore is moving AI beyond computer screens and into the physical world.

But Singapore's strategy is not simply about buying robots from foreign manufacturers. The country is also developing its own AI models, including SEA-LION and MERaLiON, designed to understand Southeast Asian languages and communication styles.

So what exactly is Singapore doing? Are its robots already operating at mass scale? And how do its homegrown AI models fit into the bigger picture?

Here is the full story.

---

## Why Singapore Is Investing in AI and Robotics

Singapore is a small country with limited land and an ageing population. It also faces the challenge of maintaining productivity while managing workforce constraints.

Robotics offers one way to address these pressures.

Machines can help perform repetitive tasks, move goods, inspect environments, and assist professionals with physically demanding work. AI can make these machines more adaptable by helping them recognize objects, understand instructions, and respond to changing conditions.

Singapore's strategy involves several areas:

- Using robots to support healthcare and medical procedures.
- Automating repetitive work in industrial and service environments.
- Testing robots in public spaces such as delivery and cleaning.
- Developing robots for public safety and hazardous operations.
- Building AI models that understand Singapore and Southeast Asia's languages and cultures.

However, not every initiative is already operating at a large scale. Some are established systems, some are pilots, and others remain in research or development.

---

## 1. Singapore's Dental Robotics: AI and Precision Surgery

Singapore has been developing AI-powered healthcare and dental technology for several years.

One notable development arrived in July 2026, when Foundation Healthcare announced Singapore's first autonomous dental implant surgical robot at its Foundation Oral Maxillofacial Surgery Centre in Farrer Park Medical Centre.

The system was developed by Beijing-based Yakebot Technology and introduced in Singapore through a partnership with Foundation Healthcare.

### What does the dental robot do?

Dental implant surgery requires careful planning and precise placement.

The robotic system combines digital treatment planning with robotic assistance during surgery. A surgeon first plans the procedure using digital imaging and then supervises the robot as it assists with the approved surgical plan.

The purpose is to improve precision and consistency, particularly in complex implant procedures.

The robot does not replace the dentist or oral surgeon. The medical professional remains responsible for diagnosis, treatment planning, supervision, and clinical decisions.

Singapore is also working on AI-powered oral health screening. In 2025, the National Dental Centre Singapore partnered with Yuyi Dental to explore AI imaging and oral health applications, including early detection of gum disease and remote dental consultations for seniors.

These developments demonstrate how AI and robotics are entering healthcare, although they do not mean every dental clinic in Singapore is using robotic surgery.

## 2. Singapore's Prison Robot: PROTECT

One of Singapore's most unusual robotics developments is taking place inside its correctional system.

In September 2026, the Singapore Prison Service introduced PROTECT, an autonomous robotic unit developed in collaboration with the Home Team Science and Technology Agency (HTX).

The robot is designed to support prison officers with supervision, surveillance, and incident response.

### What can PROTECT do?

PROTECT can patrol predefined routes autonomously.

Its AI-enabled navigation allows it to detect obstacles and move around them. During patrols, it streams live video and audio to officers at a control centre.

If an incident occurs, officers can remotely deploy the robot to provide a closer view of the situation.

It also has two-way communication capabilities, allowing officers to issue verbal instructions remotely.

The system is designed to support prison operations and provide additional situational awareness. Officers remain responsible for monitoring the robot and responding to incidents.

### Is Singapore replacing prison officers with robots?

The official purpose is to support officers, not to remove the human role entirely.

Robots can perform certain routine patrol and monitoring activities, while officers continue handling decisions, supervision, intervention, and responsibilities that require human judgment.

PROTECT is an example of an autonomous robot already introduced into Singapore's prison system. It should not be confused with the separate humanoid robot projects that are still being developed for future use.

## 3. Singapore Is Experimenting With Humanoid Robots for Public Safety

Singapore is also developing robots that look more like humans.

In September 2026, HTX opened the Home Team Humanoid Robotics Centre in One-North.

The centre is focused on developing humanoid robots for public safety operations. These machines are being trained to handle tasks that may be physically demanding, dangerous, or repetitive.

### Where could these humanoid robots be used?

Singapore is exploring several potential applications.

**Prison cell searches**

Humanoid robots could eventually assist officers in searching prison cells for contraband. Developers are investigating the fine motor control and sensing capabilities needed to handle objects in these environments.

**Post-blast investigations**

Police could use humanoid robots to examine the aftermath of explosions. A robot may be able to enter unstable or hazardous areas and help investigators inspect the scene.

**Hazardous materials**

Robots could assist civil defence officers with handling or sampling dangerous substances, reducing the need for people to enter hazardous environments.

**Ambulance restocking**

Humanoid robots may also help perform routine tasks such as restocking medical supplies in ambulances.

The centre is designed to train and test robots in realistic environments. It uses simulation, human demonstrations, and motion-capture technologies to develop robot skills.

The first humanoid robot working alongside Home Team officers in a real operational setting is targeted for 2028.

That means these humanoid applications are still being developed and validated. They are not all currently deployed in Singapore's prisons or police operations.

## 4. Singapore's Smart City Robotics: Delivery, Cleaning and Security

Singapore is not focusing only on healthcare and public safety.

The government is also developing a real-world robotics testbed at Punggol Digital District.

The initiative brings together public agencies, universities, and companies to test how autonomous robots can operate in a mixed-use urban environment.

The project is intended to explore several services:

- Food and parcel delivery.
- Security patrols.
- Cleaning.
- Coordination between robots operated by different companies.

Companies involved in the initial design work include Certis, DHL, Grab, and QuikBot.

The goal is to understand how robots can operate safely alongside people in public spaces, rather than only inside controlled factory environments.

The robots are expected to complement human workers. For example, machines could handle repetitive delivery or cleaning tasks while people focus on supervision, service management, and more complex work.

The testbed is important because a robot operating on a public path faces challenges that do not exist in a warehouse. It must navigate pedestrians, obstacles, changing weather, and different operating conditions.

Singapore is using this kind of testing to work toward robotics services that can be deployed safely and commercially.

## 5. SEA-LION: Singapore's AI Model for Southeast Asian Languages

Robotics is only one part of Singapore's AI strategy.

The country is also developing its own large language models to address a problem with global AI systems: many are less familiar with Southeast Asian languages, local expressions, and cultural contexts.

One of Singapore's major initiatives is SEA-LION.

SEA-LION is a family of open-source large language models developed by AI Singapore.

Its purpose is to make AI more useful for Southeast Asia by improving how models understand regional languages, cultures, and communication styles.

### Why does SEA-LION matter?

Southeast Asia contains many languages and dialects.

A general-purpose AI model may understand English well but struggle with regional expressions, local context, or mixed-language conversations.

SEA-LION is designed to address this gap.

The model family supports work involving regional languages and is built with an emphasis on open collaboration and adaptation.

It can help developers create AI applications for regional businesses, public services, education, and other local use cases.

SEA-LION is not itself a physical robot. It is an AI model that could support applications where language understanding and regional context are important.

---

## 6. MERaLiON: AI That Understands Speech and Communication

Singapore is also developing MERaLiON, another national AI model under its multimodal AI programme.

MERaLiON stands for Multimodal Empathetic Reasoning and Learning in One Network.

It is developed by A*STAR's Institute for Infocomm Research, with support from Singapore's national AI programme.

### What makes MERaLiON different?

MERaLiON focuses on speech and multimodal communication.

Its capabilities include:

- Understanding spoken language.
- Speech transcription and translation.
- Handling multilingual conversations.
- Recognizing communication context and certain emotional or intent-related signals.
- Supporting conversations that mix languages, such as English and Singlish.

These capabilities may be useful in customer service, healthcare communication, public services, and other applications where people do not communicate in a single formal language.

For example, an AI system supporting a multilingual service could benefit from understanding different accents, languages, and expressions.

MERaLiON is not a robot, but speech and language models could become part of the software systems used by future intelligent machines.

## 7. How SEA-LION and MERaLiON Fit Into Singapore's Robotics Strategy

It is important to understand the difference between AI models and robots.

A robot is a physical machine that can move or interact with the environment.

An AI model provides intelligence or a particular capability, such as understanding language, interpreting images, or reasoning about a task.

Singapore's strategy brings these areas together.

Robotics projects can use AI to help machines recognize their surroundings, understand instructions, and respond to events. Language models can support communication, while specialized robotics software controls movement and physical actions.

For example:

- A delivery robot may use AI to interpret its surroundings and navigate a route.
- A public safety robot may send video and audio to officers for remote monitoring.
- A humanoid robot may need AI to understand instructions before carrying out a physical task.
- A healthcare system may use speech AI to support communication between patients and professionals.

However, SEA-LION and MERaLiON should not be described as the direct control systems powering PROTECT or Singapore's humanoid robots unless that specific integration is confirmed.

Their importance is broader: they strengthen Singapore's ability to develop AI applications suited to its own languages and regional needs.

## Is Singapore Using AI Robots at Mass Scale?

Singapore is making significant progress in AI and robotics, but the phrase "mass scale" needs some qualification.

Some applications are already in operation. PROTECT has been introduced into Singapore's prison system, and robotic dental technology has been launched in a specialist healthcare setting.

Other initiatives are still being tested.

The Punggol Digital District robotics testbed is designed to evaluate how autonomous robots can work in public spaces. Humanoid robots for prison searches, hazardous materials, and post-blast investigations are being developed, with an operational target of 2028 for the first humanoid system.

Singapore's approach is therefore a combination of real deployments, pilots, and research.

It is more accurate to say that Singapore is expanding the use of AI and robotics across several sectors than to claim that the entire country has already automated its public services.

## What Happens Next?

Singapore's robotics development is likely to focus on making robots more useful outside controlled environments.

The next challenges include improving safety, reliability, navigation, and coordination between machines and people.

Public safety robots must operate reliably in sensitive environments. Delivery robots must navigate public spaces without creating hazards. Medical robotics must meet clinical requirements and remain under appropriate professional supervision.

The country is also continuing to develop its own AI models, supporting local research, regional language capabilities, and applications across Southeast Asia.

As these technologies mature, Singapore could become an important place for testing and deploying AI-powered machines in healthcare, public safety, logistics, and urban services.

But the key question is not simply how many robots Singapore can introduce. It is whether these systems can perform useful work safely, reliably, and in ways that genuinely support people.

## Conclusion

Singapore is building a broader AI and robotics ecosystem that goes beyond the United States and China.

Its developments include robotic dental surgery, the PROTECT prison patrol robot, humanoid systems being developed for public safety, and plans to test delivery and cleaning robots in a smart urban district.

At the same time, Singapore is investing in homegrown AI models such as SEA-LION and MERaLiON to improve regional language understanding and support local AI applications.

These are different technologies, but they form part of a wider strategy: bringing AI into real-world services while building the country's own research and engineering capabilities.

Singapore has not automated every sector, and many humanoid applications remain experimental. Nevertheless, its combination of existing robotic deployments, public-sector testing, healthcare innovation, and regional AI research makes it an important country to watch in the next phase of physical AI.
